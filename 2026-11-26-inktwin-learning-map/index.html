Start here · 60 seconds

Teach a small AI to write in your handwriting.

InkTwin is a hobby research project. You write a few words with a pen tablet. A small computer program studies how your pen moved. Then it writes new words that look like you wrote them, in English and one day in Telugu. It builds on a published project called PenGPT, which this page calls the reference model.

1. You writeA few words on a pen tablet. The page records every pen movement.
2. A model studiesIt practises guessing your next pen move, over and over, until it gets good.
3. It writes like youType any new word. The model writes it in your style, stroke by stroke.

The reference model mostly learned from traced scans

About 98% of PenGPT's 495,590 training words were scans turned into strokes by another model [V]. Your real pen recordings are a true point of difference.

The released model cannot write Telugu

Its Telugu text inputs were never trained [V]. It runs without errors and draws meaningless strokes.

No public Telugu word-level pen data

Only isolated characters exist (HP Labs, 166 classes) [V]. Your recordings would be among the first.

Three writers can demo, not prove

Family data can test the recording app and show personalisation. It cannot train or fairly judge a general model. The reference test needs at least 16 unseen writers [V].

Start with the big picture

The big picture

In plain words: a pen stroke passes through seven stations on its way from your hand to new handwriting made by a model.

Try this Tap each station, left to right.

What you just saw Each station depends on the one before it. A problem early on, such as fake pressure readings, cannot be repaired later.

The key idea: a chatbot guesses the next word. InkTwin's model guesses the next pen movement. It is autocomplete for pen movements.

Because the result is a list of pen moves and not a picture, it can be replayed like a video, drawn at any size, and studied stroke by stroke.

Why this matters for you

This is why the plan starts with a boring-sounding device test and leaves the exciting model training for much later. Good data first.

Go deeper: what each readout means, and the full device checklist
  • Input type is the browser's pointerType: pen, mouse or touch. For real data it must say pen.
  • Pressure runs from 0 to 1. The web standard uses 0.5 as the filler when a device has no pressure sensor [V].
  • Lean (tilt) reads 0 when the device does not report it [V], so 0 is not proof the pen is upright.
  • Points per second: the tablet is rated at 200 reports per second [V]. Whether the browser delivers every one, each with its own timestamp, is unknown [NV].
  • In-between points are coalesced events. The page asks for them with getCoalescedEvents(), which only works on secure pages (https or localhost) [V].
  • The Windows Ink behaviour above comes from user reports and bug trackers, and has not been tested on this tablet yet [NV].

Milestone 0 test list

  1. Input type, pressure, tilt in Chrome, Edge and Firefox, with Windows Ink on and off, over USB cable and Bluetooth.
  2. Points per second, and whether their timestamps differ.
  3. How many different pressure values appear in one slow press and release.
  4. Whether hovering is reported, and what the pen's side buttons do.
  5. Real precision in full-screen and windowed mode, by drawing a zigzag about 1 mm tall.
  6. Timestamp precision with and without the special security headers (COOP and COEP).
  7. Comfort: can each family member write naturally while looking at the screen?
Why this matters for you

The review calls "silent default values" a critical risk. If you skip this test you could collect weeks of family handwriting with fake pressure and not notice.

Ink into tokens

In plain words: a model cannot read a wobbly line, so we rebuild the line from a tiny alphabet of moves, like building a curve out of Lego bricks. Each brick is called a token.

The same ink as a list of tokens

Grey line: your ink. Coloured line: the same ink snapped to the grid. Each colour is one of 8 compass directions. DOWN means the pen touches the surface, UP means it lifts. Faded arrows are moves in the air between strokes.

What you just saw Small bricks follow your line closely but need many tokens. Big bricks need few tokens but look blocky. The tool that does this conversion is a tokenizer.

What you just saw A real merging algorithm running on your own drawing. Common pen gestures, such as "keep going right", became single bricks, so the same word needs fewer tokens.

Go deeper: what the reference tokenizer really does
  • Points are snapped to a grid of 0.02 units, where a lowercase letter is 0.123 units tall [V].
  • 10 base tokens: 8 directions plus DOWN and UP [V].
  • 512 learned merges, which never cross a DOWN or UP [V]. The demo above follows the same rule.
  • Full vocabulary: 10 base + 512 merges + 4 special tokens = 526 [V].
  • Time, pressure and tilt are thrown away [V].
  • The 8-direction alphabet is not specific to English letters, so it works for Telugu unchanged [V]. Merges learned on English fit Telugu's loops less well, so the review suggests learning merges on a balanced mix of both scripts [Rec].
  • This design is not new. ScribeTokens published the 8-direction plus BPE idea in March 2026 [V]. Do not claim it as yours.
Why this matters for you

Milestone 0.5 asks you to turn your own test ink into tokens and back, and measure how much is lost. Rebuilding this tokenizer yourself is the best first coding exercise in the project.

Numbers in, guesses out

In plain words: inside the model everything is numbers. Each token is swapped for a short row of numbers, sums are done, and out comes a score for every possible next move.

What you just saw The row is called an embedding. The grid is called a tensor, which is just a spreadsheet of numbers. Moves that point in similar directions got similar rows. A real model discovers such patterns by itself during training.

Scores become chances, and chances become a loss

What you just saw The raw scores are called logits. A formula called softmax turns them into chances that add up to 100%. The loss is how surprised the model was by the right answer. "Practise once" nudged every score in the direction that lowers the loss. That nudge is all that training is.

Go deeper: the two formulas

Softmax: chance of move i = escore i ÷ (sum of escore over all moves). Raising one score lowers every other chance.

Cross-entropy loss: loss = −ln(chance given to the correct move). Ten equal chances give −ln(0.1) ≈ 2.30. A 90% chance gives 0.11. The unit is the nat.

The nudge: for softmax with cross-entropy, the slope for each score is simply (its chance − 1) for the correct move and (its chance) for every other move. "Practise once" subtracts 0.6 × slope from each score. This is real gradient descent on one example.

PenGPT's embedding rows are 208 numbers long and its vocabulary has 526 tokens [V]. This demo uses 12 numbers and 10 tokens, with made-up values.

PenGPT reports its loss as nats per pen move: 0.711 for its base model, and 0.786 for unseen writers after 3 example words [V].

Why this matters for you

When you train your own model in Milestone 5, the one number you will watch all day is this loss. Now you know what it measures.

Go deeper: how the toy works, and what the real loop adds

The toy keeps a table of 100 rows (every pair of previous moves) × 10 columns (every next move). Each round it shuffles the practice examples, takes them 8 at a time (a batch), computes the softmax loss slope for the rows involved, and subtracts 2.0 × the average slope. One pass through all examples is one epoch. The last 20% of the examples are never used for practice and give the "fresh-data" loss.

The sample data is the same word drawn at three slightly different sizes. Making altered copies of your data like this is called data augmentation.

A real loop in PyTorch has the same four lines: get a batch, compute the loss, compute the gradients (PyTorch does this automatically), take a step. The step is usually taken by an optimizer called Adam, which adjusts the step size for each number separately.

PenGPT's base model trains in 25 minutes on one A100 graphics chip [V]. The review estimates your own model at 2 to 8 hours per run on the laptop [Rec].

Why this matters for you

This toy is close to the very first model the review tells you to build in Milestone 5: a simple counter of "which move follows which". You have just run it.

Go deeper: the review's rules for honest splits
  • Public data: split by writer into training, validation and test writers. Add a per-writer word split for "known writer" scores [Rec].
  • Family data: example words come only from session-1 reference prompts. Target words come only from held-out prompts. Never let a prompt appear in both [Rec].
  • PenGPT held out 63 writers: in every source with at least 20 writers, those whose ID hashes to 0 modulo 20 [V].
  • With tiny per-writer data, fine-tuning will memorise. Check generated words against the training ink using a nearest-neighbour distance (DTW) [Rec].
  • Report random, unselected samples [Rec].
Why this matters for you

This is the reason the 72 family sentences cannot train a model. They are far too few, and three relatives cannot stand in for "a person the model has never met". The family data is for testing the app and for personalising a model trained on hundreds of public writers.

What you just saw Two kinds of looking. Self-attention looks back at earlier pen moves, mostly the recent ones and where the stroke began. Cross-attention looks across at the letter being written and at the writer slot. The model can never look at moves that have not happened yet.

Go deeper: the reference model's real numbers
  • Released PenGPT handwriting model: 6,077,360 parameters, 8 layers, 208 wide, 4 attention heads, 256-token context [V]. The paper's comparison experiments use a smaller 5.2M model, 192 wide [V].
  • It is a decoder-only Transformer trained with cross-entropy loss. Each block has causal self-attention, cross-attention to a context sequence, and a ReLU² MLP (pengpt.py, class Block, lines 943–956) [V].
  • Extras: RoPE position encoding, QK-norm, logit soft-capping, and sine/cosine pen-position features added to the token embeddings [V].
  • The side notes are one learned writer vector followed by the text as embedded UTF-8 bytes. There is no separate text-reading model [V].
  • The counter's formula: 16 × width² per layer (4 for self-attention, 4 for cross-attention, 8 for the MLP) plus embedding tables for 526 tokens, 256 byte values and 1,193 writer slots, plus the output table. At 8 layers and width 208 it gives about 6.06 million, close to the real 6,077,360.
  • The review's suggestion for your own model: about 4–6M parameters, 6–8 layers, width 192–256, 4 heads, context 256–512 tokens. Your data (about 130k English words, an estimate) argues for smaller, not larger [Rec].
Why this matters for you

You do not need a giant model or an expensive rented machine. The review is blunt: a model of this size on your laptop's 6 GB graphics chip is the right scale.

Writing new ink, one move at a time

In plain words: a trained model writes by rolling a weighted dice for the next move, adding that move to the page, and rolling again, until it decides the word is finished.

Try this Tap "Next move" a few times, then "Auto-play". Restart with the temperature at 2.0 and see the letter fall apart.

Watch a letter being written
Chances for the next move
Moves written so far

The scores for each step are scripted for this demo (the "model" wants to draw a letter e). The dice roll, the temperature and the top-k cut are real and happen in your browser.

What you just saw This is sampling. Temperature squashes or stretches the chances before the dice roll. Top-k removes unlikely moves from the dice completely. The special END token is how the model says "this word is done".

Go deeper: from tokens back to smooth handwriting
  • Temperature divides every score before softmax. Below 1 it sharpens the favourite, above 1 it flattens the chances.
  • After generation, the blocky grid walk is turned back into curves by corner detection plus Chaikin smoothing (strokes.py, function curve) [V].
  • Generating with a trained model, without changing it, is called inference. PenGPT's handwriting inference runs with NumPy alone, and in the browser [V].
  • PenGPT's samples at full temperature are read correctly less often than real ink: 54% against 66% [V].
Why this matters for you

The public demo in Milestone 7 is exactly this loop, running in a visitor's browser, with your own handwriting style as the starting point.

Learning from a handful of examples is called few-shot learning. Placing the examples in front is called priming. The catch is space. The model can only look at a limited number of tokens at once, its context window.

Three ways to personalise
Go deeper: how the reference model was built for this, and how it was tested
  • The context window is 256 tokens, and the paper notes that even 3 priming words rarely fit without BPE [V]. The review's advice: priming for up to 3 words, a fitted writer vector or light fine-tuning for 5 to 10 [Rec].
  • "Tokens per word" above is this page's guess, not a figure from the review. Measure yours in Milestone 0.5.
  • During training, each sequence groups 1–4 words by the same writer, separated by two WORD tokens. The writer vector is swapped for the "unknown" one half of the time, which forces the model to read style from the ink itself [V].
  • Proof that the prefix does the work: a version with no writer vectors at all still ranks the true writer 1.8 on average with 3 priming words [V].
  • The test (style rank): take a word by an unseen writer. Score it after 16 different prefixes: the true writer's 3 words and 3 words from each of 15 other unseen writers. Rank 1 is best, pure chance is 8.5. Result: 1.9 after 3 words, 2.55 after 1 word [V].
  • This measures how well the model predicts the real ink. It does not score how good generated samples look [V].
  • Few-shot handwriting is well studied (DSD, SDT, DiffInk, PenGPT) [V]. Comparing the three methods is a good learning project, not a discovery.
Why this matters for you

Comparing the three methods with 1, 3, 5 and 10 example words is a complete small experiment. The review estimates it at about an hour on your laptop.

The whole journey

What you just saw Almost everything runs on your own laptop. The cloud is optional. The model that does the writing is trained on hundreds of public writers. Your family's ink is used to personalise and to test, not to train from scratch.

One firm rule from the review: a model that writes like your mother or father is a forgery tool for cheques and forms. Keep family models private. Anything public uses your own handwriting only.

What you just saw Each English letter takes 1 byte. Each stored Telugu character takes 3, and one visible Telugu letter can be built from several stored characters. The reference model allows only 50 bytes per word, so long Telugu words are thrown out.

Joining consonants

క+్+ష=క్ష

The small mark in the middle is the virama. It joins two consonants. The second one often shrinks to a small shape below, called an ottu. Three stored characters, one visible letter.

Vowel signs

క+ి=కి

A vowel after a consonant is not a separate letter. It is a sign attached above, below or beside it. There are hundreds of combinations, far more than 12 sentences can cover.

The pen goes back

1 main letters of the word2 go back: vowel signs3 go back: ottus

Telugu writers often add the signs after the main letters, sometimes after the whole word. So the order of the pen is not the order of reading. Working one word at a time copes with this.

Why the released model cannot write Telugu

The model gives every byte an embedding. Bytes it practised with have large, meaningful ones. Bytes it never saw still hold tiny random numbers.

Telugu bytes

It accepts Telugu text without complaint and then draws meaningless strokes.

Go deeper: the exact numbers, and what the review recommends for Telugu
  • PenGPT drops any word label longer than 50 bytes [V]. A Telugu code point is 3 bytes, so about 16 fit.
  • Embedding size (norm): about 93 for printable English bytes, about 10.9 for every byte above 0x7F, which equals random initialisation. Telugu's lead bytes 0xE0, 0xB0 and 0xB1 measure 10.1 to 11.4 [V].
  • 36 consonants × about 13 vowel signs is about 470 combinations, plus hundreds of conjuncts [Rec]. Aim to cover all vowels, consonants, vowel signs and the 30–40 most common ottus.
  • Delayed strokes are plausible but not verified [Rec, NV].
  • "Visible letters" are grapheme clusters. Since Unicode 15.1 the virama links consonants into one cluster; older software splits them [V]. Record which Unicode version your tools use. A cluster is not always the same as a Telugu akshara.
  • Replace bytes with a shared list of code points: English characters plus the Telugu block U+0C00 to U+0C7F, about 225 symbols, plus a script tag. Raise the text limit to about 64 symbols [Rec].
  • Store text in NFC form and keep the raw text too [V].
  • Measure Telugu letter size by the body height of the main consonants, not by English x-height [Rec].
  • Train on English first, then continue on Telugu, and compare with Telugu-only training. That comparison is itself a result [Rec].
  • Telugu data, in order of preference: your own consented recordings; HP Labs isolated characters (research only, cannot be shared on); pseudo-ink traced from CC BY word images, clearly labelled as synthetic [Rec].
Why this matters for you

Telugu is the adventurous part of the project and the part most likely to be new. There is no shortcut through the released model. The honest goal for Milestone 6 is a report on what worked and what did not.

Dataset explorer

Just dots of colour. The order of the strokes is gone. Software can guess it (derendering), but a guess is not a recording.

What you just saw English is well covered for research use. Telugu pen recordings are almost absent, and the only Telugu set that may be freely shared is made of photos from just 11 writers.

Go deeper: is there enough, and what may be published?

Enough English? Yes, for non-commercial research. IAM-OnDB, BRUSH and the new part of DeepWriting give roughly 485 writers with writer IDs [V]. Every one carries a non-commercial licence, so decide now that InkTwin is a non-commercial research project.

Most of the reference model's data was photos. About 98% of PenGPT's words were scans traced into strokes by a model called InkSight. Real tablet notes were 2,084 words [V].

  • Private research: all sets are fine within their terms.
  • Publishing a trained model: a grey area for research-only sets. Use a non-commercial licence and ask the owners.
  • Publishing data made from them: not from HPL, IAM or BRUSH. Yes from IIIT-INDIC-HW-WORDS (CC BY) and your own consented recordings.
  • Demo: cleanest when built on your own consented handwriting.

Combining collections means converting each to the same units and letter size. Expect about a day per collection plus several days of cleaning [Rec]. Timing is unreliable across sources, so do not feed timing to the model.

Why this matters for you

Your own recordings are the only Telugu pen data you can ever release. That makes the family pilot, small as it is, the seed of something that does not exist yet.

Example prompts and test prompts

Before anyone writes, sort each language's sentences into two piles. Example sentences are shown to the model. Test sentences are held out: the model must write them without ever having seen that person write them. Keeping the piles apart stops the model from peeking at the answer.

Make it easy on your parents

  • They only write. You do the cutting into words and the checking afterwards.
  • Swap the language order for the next person, to balance tiredness.
  • Let them scribble first. Writing on a pad while watching a screen feels odd.
  • Ask both parents whether the Telugu sentences sound natural.
  • Save a backup file after every sitting.

What 3 people can show

  • The recording app works with real users.
  • A demo of each person's style that you can look at.
  • How much one person's writing varies from day to day.
  • How each person's English and Telugu styles relate.
  • A 3-way "who wrote this?" test, where blind guessing scores 33%.

What 3 people cannot prove

  • That the model works for people it has never seen.
  • Reliable differences in style or readability scores.
  • That Telugu generation works.
  • The reference model's main test, which needs 16 unseen writers.
  • "Family style" apart from "personal style". Relatives may write alike.
Go deeper: the review's exact protocol
  • 2 sessions per person on different days, about 20 minutes each, about 40 minutes in total [Rec].
  • 12 English sentences of 6–9 words and 12 Telugu sentences of 4–7 words: about 90 English and 65 Telugu words per person [Rec, estimate].
  • Session 1: 3 minutes of warm-up scribbling (logged, not saved as data), then 6 English and 6 Telugu sentences. Session 2: the other 6 + 6, plus 2 repeats from session 1, one per language [Rec].
  • Split the 12 prompts per language into 6 reference and 6 held-out prompts before collection [Rec].
  • "Minutes per sentence" is this page's own setting, chosen so the default matches about 20 minutes per session.
  • Consent is recorded with separate yes/no answers for: collecting, research use, public release, and a public model of my style. Include a way to delete [Rec].
  • Screenless-tablet comfort is rated a high risk. Optionally photograph one paper sample per person so "naturalness" can be judged by eye [Rec].
Safety rule from the review: a working generator of a parent's handwriting is a forgery tool. Keep family models private. A public demo should use your own handwriting only.

What you just saw The most original idea is also the hardest: using someone's English handwriting to produce their Telugu, or the reverse. The safest original contribution is the data itself: the same people writing in both scripts.

Go deeper: how to describe the project honestly

The review's suggested one-line description [Rec]: "A same-writer English–Telugu online handwriting pilot corpus, and an empirical study of few-shot and cross-script personalization of a compact pen-token Transformer trained on real trajectories."

  • Novelty scores rest on a literature search that did not fully cover IEEE Xplore or the ICFHR/ICDAR conferences. Treat them as provisional.
  • Do not claim the tokenizer or few-shot priming as new. Both are published [V].
  • Before writing "first Telugu ...", spend about an hour searching IEEE Xplore and Semantic Scholar.
  • Present it as a small-scale study.
